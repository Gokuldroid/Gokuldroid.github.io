> Your manager's manager knowing your work firsthand is insurance against the single point of failure that is your manager.

## The single point of failure

Strip away the org chart pleasantries and here is your actual situation: your entire organizational existence is mediated through one person. Your manager decides what fraction of your work gets mentioned upward, how it gets framed, whose name is attached to it, and how hard to push when your case comes up. Everyone above your manager knows you only through that filter.

If your manager is strong, engaged, and generous with credit, this works fine. But you are exposed to four failure modes, and none of them are your fault:

- **Weak advocate.** Your manager is a poor storyteller or has low credibility with their own peers. Your work gets under-sold in every room it enters.
- **Overloaded.** Your manager has twelve reports and three fires. Your narrative simply doesn't get airtime — not out of malice, out of bandwidth.
- **Leaving.** Your manager quits, transfers, or gets re-orged. Everything they knew about you walks out the door with them.
- **Credit hoarding.** Your work gets presented as "the team's work" or, worse, theirs. Above your manager, you don't exist as an individual at all.

In any of these cases, your work — however technically excellent — does not exist above your manager. Not "is undervalued." Does not exist. There is no record of it in the minds that make decisions about you.

A skip-level relationship — your manager's manager knowing your name, your work, and your trajectory from direct observation — is redundancy for this single point of failure. Engineers build redundancy for every critical system except their own careers.

## Mechanics

**Manager churn is the norm, not the exception.** Re-orgs, departures, team splits: the average tenure of any given manager *over you* is often barely a year. Each transition wipes your narrative. The new manager inherits a spreadsheet, not a story. They didn't see you carry the migration; they see a name and a level. Unless someone above the churn holds continuity, you restart your reputation from zero every cycle. The skip is that continuity. Managers rotate underneath them; the skip's memory of you persists across the rotations, and they brief incoming managers: "this one's strong, don't lose them."

**Promotions are decided in rooms where your skip sits.** Your manager advocates; the room decides. In a calibration or promo discussion, your manager's claims are, structurally, claims from an interested party. When your skip can second the case from direct knowledge — "I watched that design review, the questions were sharp, the system has held up" — that is testimony from someone with no incentive to inflate. It is worth more than any promo packet, because packets are written by advocates and everyone in the room knows it. This is the mentor-vs-sponsor distinction in its purest form: a mentor advises you privately; a sponsor spends their credibility on you in rooms you're not in. The skip is the most natural sponsor you can cultivate, because they already have standing in exactly the rooms that decide your outcomes.

**Skips control the scope pool.** New initiatives, charters, and headline projects are carved up at skip level *before* managers ever hear of them. By the time your manager is asked "who do you have for this," the shape of the opportunity is fixed. If the skip already knows your name when the carving happens — "have Priya's team take the latency workstream, she's got that engineer who did the cache rewrite" — you get routed scope you'd never have seen otherwise. Being unknown at skip level means competing only for the scraps that trickle down.

## How to build it cleanly

The failure mode to avoid is obvious: going around your manager. Done wrong, a skip relationship reads as politicking and damages the relationship you depend on daily. Done right, it's invisible and your manager benefits from it too.

**Use official channels first.** If your skip offers skip-level 1:1s or office hours, take them — most engineers treat these as optional and skip them, which is exactly why they're leverage. Bring substance, not status updates: one concrete thing you're seeing on the ground that the skip can't see from their altitude (a friction point, a risk, a pattern across teams), and one genuine question about org direction. Status is what your manager already reports; ground truth and good questions are what a skip remembers.

**Make your artifacts travel upward.** Write crisp, forwardable summaries of your results — three sentences, outcome first — that your manager can paste into their own update with zero editing. This is managing-up mechanics: you're making your manager's advocacy job effortless, and your name rides along. Then perform in the skip's direct line of sight: demos, design reviews, incident reviews where they're present. Firsthand observation beats any forwarded summary — this is airtime and the demo effect working for you at the level where it compounds most.

**Never use skip access to complain about your manager.** The exception is a genuine integrity issue — those you escalate, full stop. But routine friction, style disagreements, "I don't feel supported"? Skips read complaints about your manager as information about *your* character first and your manager's second. You've marked yourself as someone who escalates instead of resolving.

**Keep your manager informed.** Mention skip interactions casually and afterward: "Had my skip 1:1, we talked about the platform roadmap." No-surprises applies in both directions — you expect it from your manager, extend it upward too. The goal is redundancy, not bypass. A manager who feels bypassed becomes an adversary; a manager who sees their skip praising their report gets credit for developing you.

## Timing

Build it before you need it. A skip who first hears your name during a promo debate, a re-org, or a conflict cannot help you — they have no data, and anything they say is speculation. A skip who has watched you deliver for a year can end a debate with one sentence. Insurance bought during the fire pays nothing.

## Diagnostic test

If your manager resigned tomorrow, could your skip describe two specific things you shipped this year — without asking anyone?
